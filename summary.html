The 100 prisoners puzzle, and what it can do for a campaign

It starts with a famous puzzle about 100 prisoners, 100 boxes and a loop trick that beats impossible odds. The marketing idea is to use that trick to run a single-product campaign. Group the audience's IP addresses into circles, rotate through them, and cut the impressions that don't convert. This page summarises what the simulations found and when the idea works.

The short answer

Yes, under the right conditions. With the default settings it roughly doubled sales from the same budget and halved the waste. The gain doesn't come from the prisoners' loop itself. It comes from moving budget toward circles that sell and resting IPs that just saw the ad. It only works if your circles really do convert differently, and only a split test can prove that for your campaign.

1 · The puzzle

The 100 prisoners conundrum

The setup

100 prisoners are numbered 1 to 100. In a sealed room stand 100 boxes, also numbered 1 to 100. The warden has put the prisoners' numbers into the boxes at random, one number per box. One at a time, each prisoner enters, opens up to 50 boxes looking for their own number, then leaves the room exactly as they found it. Nobody can signal or talk to anyone afterwards. If every prisoner finds their own number, all 100 go free. If even one fails, they all stay. They may agree on a plan beforehand.

The obvious approach fails

If each prisoner opens 50 boxes at random, each has a 50% chance. The prisoners are independent, so all 100 succeed only with the chance ½ multiplied by itself a hundred times.

The solution: follow the loop

Every prisoner uses the same three steps:

  • Start at your own box. Prisoner 17 first opens box 17.
  • Go where the number sends you. If box 17 holds 42, open box 42 next.
  • Repeat until you find your own number, or you've opened 50 boxes.

Why it works

Every box points to exactly one other box, and every box is pointed to by exactly one other. Following the pointers can't branch or dead-end, so the boxes split into closed loops. The box holding your number is the one that points back to your starting box. Start at your own box and you're always on the loop that contains your number, so you're certain to reach it. You reach it within 50 openings if and only if your loop has 50 boxes or fewer.

So everyone goes free exactly when no loop is longer than 50. The prisoners no longer succeed or fail on their own. Everyone on a short loop wins, and everyone on the one long loop, if there is one, loses.

The maths behind the 31%

At most one loop can be longer than 50, since two would need more than 100 boxes. In a random arrangement of 100 boxes, the chance of a loop of exactly length L, for any L above 50, is simply 1/L. Adding those up gives the chance of failure:

1/51 + 1/52 + … + 1/100 ≈ 0.688

That leaves about 31.2% for success. With more prisoners (and half as many openings each) the figure settles at 1 − ln 2 ≈ 30.7%, and it never falls below that.

Two things worth knowing

  • No one's personal odds change. Each prisoner still finds their number exactly half the time. The strategy wins by making the outcomes move together, which only helps when success is all or nothing.
  • A spiteful warden can be beaten. If the warden knows the plan and arranges one giant loop, the prisoners can secretly agree a random relabelling of the boxes beforehand. That restores the 31%.

From the puzzle to marketing

A campaign counts total sales, not "everyone or no one", so the correlation effect on its own adds nothing there. What carries over is the structure: organising the search into loops, rotating through them, and letting each result tell you where to look next. That is the idea tested in the rest of this page.

2 · The marketing problem

One product, an audience of IPs, a fixed daily budget

The audience is a set of IP addresses grouped into circles, such as /24 subnets or local areas. Each day you can afford a fixed number of impressions. Most impressions don't convert; those are the failures. Two facts shape the problem:

  • Circles differ. Some circles convert much better than others, and neighbouring circles tend to be alike.
  • Repeats wear out. Showing the same IP again within a few days converts less well (ad fatigue).
3 · What the simulation found

Same budget, twice the sales

20,000 IPs in 100 circles, 500 impressions a day for 30 days, averaged over 10 simulated campaigns. Every strategy ran on the same audiences.

Rotation alone did nothing. The budget never reaches every IP, so a random blast rarely repeats anyone anyway. Following hot circles did most of the work: in one campaign it put 59% of the budget into the best fifth of circles, which convert 3.2× the average. The retry loop added the rest by stopping the hot circles from being hammered.

1

Circles must convert differently

If every circle converts about the same, there's no hot circle to find. This matters most.

2

Hot circles must stay hot

A circle that sold well last week has to keep selling well this week, or following it is chasing noise.

3

Enough sales to learn from

Every sale is a data point. With very few, the strategy can't tell hot circles from lucky ones.

4

Neighbours alike (a bonus)

When side-by-side subnets behave alike, one hot circle points to others, and the strategy learns faster.

5

Fatigue decides the retry loop

If repeats lose impact, rest IPs between showings. If they don't, resting good IPs wastes them, so skip it.

5 · Checking a real campaign

Look back first, then test forward

The diagnosis page takes a finished campaign as either an impression log (date, ip, converted) or daily totals per circle. It tests each condition above and estimates the gain. On sample data with known settings it gets both cases right:

A good diagnosis is a reason to test, not proof. To settle it, split your circles into two random halves. Run hot circles + retry on one half and your usual targeting on the other, with the same budget, for 2–4 weeks. Aim for at least 100 sales per half, then compare cost per sale.

6 · Risks

Before running this for real

  • IP addresses are personal data in the EU and UK. Targeting by IP needs a legal basis. Working from circle totals rather than individual IPs reduces what you have to keep.
  • IPs are messy. Homes, offices and mobile carriers share addresses, and many change often. Circles hold up better than single IPs, but the signal is blurrier than in the simulation.
  • Attribution is the hard part. The strategy needs each sale tied back to a circle. Browsers and privacy rules increasingly block that tracking.
  • It isn't new. Shifting budget to what converts and capping frequency are standard; ad platforms already do versions of both. The value here is doing it deliberately at the circle level, and checking the conditions first.
